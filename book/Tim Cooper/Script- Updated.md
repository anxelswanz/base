## Scene 2

Sheffield is situated at the confluence of five rivers, where abundant wood resources and sandstones—combined with deep wooded valleys and rapid streams—provided charcoal for smelting, fuel for forges, and waterpower for driving hammers and grindstones.





## scene 3

By the early 17 century, three out of every five men in the region were working as cutlers. They had a special name for these people:  Little Mesters. They were not salaried factory workers, but **independent micro-contractors** who rented their own workshop space, bought their own tools, set their own prices, and worked for themselves.

So before the 19th century, a Sheffield cutler didn't view himself as a "worker" at all. He owned his tools, stamped his name on the blades, and controlled his own schedule. He was his own boss—and even if he wasn't yet, he knew he would be soon. And that wasn't just some pipe dream; a low-level assistant really could work his way up to becoming a Little Mester.

In 1819, a local historian named Joseph Hunter wrote: **"There is little elegance in Sheffield, but there is a fierce spirit of independence, and a rough honesty."** He even added that the town had very little crime and people got along pretty well.



## Scene 4 

Historically, Sheffield has long been regarded as a city with a rebellious, radical, and independent streak. King George III of Britain once described Sheffield as a "damned bad place." That rebel spirit didn't start with politics—it started in church. The people of Sheffield flat-out refused to toe the line for the Church of England. Instead, they joined independent religious movements, earning themselves a name: **Nonconformists**. Literally, "the ones who refuse to conform." 

Beyond its religious defiance, Sheffield was also **one of the leading cities** to take a stand against the slave trade. As early as 1789, **more than 700 Sheffield metalworkers signed and submitted a petition to Parliament demanding the total abolition of the slave trade**. This was an astonishing act, given that these working men didn't even have the right to vote at the time.

Among the city's most prominent figures was **James Montgomery**, whose statue stands proudly in the City Centre Sheffield. For decades, he fought tirelessly for two groups: slaves chained across the Atlantic and young chimney boys  shoved into narrow flues. For those who had no voice.

Another trailblazer was **Mary Anne Rawson**, who co-founded the **Sheffield Female Anti-Slavery Society**. Through speeches, publications, and campaigns, she called on the public to boycott goods produced by slave labor in the West Indies, such as sugar, coffee, and cotton.

So by the end of the eighteenth century, Sheffield already had two things.

It had an "I" — a man who owned his tools, stamped his name into the steel, and for himself.

And it had a "We" — chapels, committees, petitions, societies. But look at who that "We" spoke for. Slaves chained across an ocean. Boys shoved up chimneys. It pointed outward — or those who had no voice.

What happened made we turned around to themselves.


## SCENE 5  1791 


"The outstanding fact of the period between 1790 and 1830 is the formation of 'the working class'... the consciousness of an identity of interests as between all these diverse groups of working people and as against the interests of other classes."

That is a quote from historian E.P. Thompson, and it perfectly captures the moment English society tore into two distinct sides: "We," the ordinary working people, and "They," the aristocrats, the squires, and Parliament. And Sheffield was on the absolute frontlines.

It started in 1789 when the French Revolution broke out, and Sheffield reacted with wild celebration. Soon, Thomas Paine’s _Rights of Man_ spread like wildfire among local craftsmen, the "Little Mesters." By late 1791, they took a step further, establishing the **Sheffield Society for Constitutional Information** to demand universal male suffrage and an end to a corrupt Parliament. Then "They", the ruling class, made their move.

In the same year, Parliament passed an Enclosure Act, privatising nearly six thousand acres of common land — where the poor had grazed animals and gathered firewood for centuries.

The city went wild. One group marched straight to Broom Hall and burnt it to the ground. That was the home of James Wilkinson who was a local vicar and magistrate. In 1795, driven by extreme hardship and soaring food prices, the people gathered to protest on Norfolk Street. The army fired directly into the crowd, killing two people in response. When James Montgomery, the editor of the _Sheffield Iris_, reported on the Norfolk Street shooting, they threw him in prison. 

The government then went a step further, setting up a permanent military garrison in Sheffield—specifically to suppress the people of this city. To completely silence the dissent, Parliament weaponized the law: first the **"Two Acts" of 1795** banning large gatherings, followed by the **Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800**, which made worker solidarity a literal crime.


## SCENE 6 · Paradise Square 1832


If you go to Paradise Square today, it’s just a quiet cobblestone slope surrounded by old buildings. But once, twenty thousand people stood right in this place.

It started with the Reform Act of 1832. Sheffield finally got its own MPs, ending a long history of tens of thousands of people having zero representation. It sounded like a victory. But the act also set strict rules for voting: you had to own property of a certain value, or pay at least ten pounds a year in rent. The vast majority of workers qualified for neither.

To make matters worse, the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 introduced the notorious workhouse system where families were torn apart and **forced into grueling labor under prison-like conditions**.

That crushing disappointment quickly fueled the Chartist movement. In October 1837, the Sheffield Working Men's Association was formed to fight for true political rights. However, the authorities met this growing resistance with a heavy hand. Armed police patrols roamed the town centre, ambushing suspected Chartists, while police and military forces moved in to suppress rallies right here in Paradise Square, leading to over 79 arrests.

The Chartist movement didn't win the vote in the end. But before the movement, the question was: "Do workers even deserve the vote?" After the movement, the question changed to: "When, and how many of them can vote?"

## SCENE 7 Industrial Revolution and We as working class

By 1851, with 52 percent of men and 17 percent of women working in metal trades, Sheffield had a greater proportion of its industrial workforce than any other town in the country. 

In Sheffield both masters and artisans came from the same social background and there was an absence of the rigid social distinction apparent in other industrial towns, particularly the factory-based centres such as Manchester and Leeds. 

This meant there wasn't a huge gap between the rich and the poor because these 'Little Mesters' would often hire outworkers for one job, and then turn around and work for someone else the next.

But the Industrial Revolution changed everything. A massive surge in global orders demanded speed and scale, so a single craftsman couldn't possibly forge a knife from start to finish anymore.

The workers morphed into cogs in a machine—trapped as outworkers, earning depressed wages, locked in a dependency by those who controlled the global market. In times of price depression, manufacturers kept rents artificially high, forcing workers into endless debt. This was the vile practice of "pawning the workers." 

And to seal their fate, there was the infamous **Master and Servant Act of 1823**. Under this law, if a worker left without consent, slacked off, or dared to strike, **it was treated not as a breach of contract, but as a criminal offense.** 

Tim Cooper comments in **_The Story of Sheffield_**, "This was a profound disrespect to the independent cutlers and stood in stark contrast to Sheffield's proud heyday.

## SCENE 8 

Founded in 1858, the Sheffield Trade Union Council united various independent trade unions into a cohesive whole to build collective strength.

During the 1850s and 1860s, to enforce compliance with union rules, trade unions routinely resorted to a brutal tactic **"Rattening"**—sabotage, intimidation, and violent attacks. This dark era became known as the **Sheffield Outrages**.

The violence peaked on the rainy night of October 8, 1866, when union agents sneaked into a house on Hereford Street, dropped a canister of gunpowder down the chimney, and detonated it. Although the blast destroyed the building, the occupant miraculously crawled out of the rubble alive.

The Hereford Street bombing was more than just a criminal offense; it acted as a mirror, warning the ruling class that completely blocking the legitimate demands and negotiation channels of the  masses will easily breed underground, terrorist-style resistance on the social margins.

Yet, this tragedy ultimately served as a positive catalyst: the **Trade Union Act of 1871** was passed, officially granting trade unions legal status and protecting their funds.

But financial protection was not enough, because a silent, insidious weapon still hung over every worker: **Criminal Conspiracy**. Under this legal logic, if you alone decided not to go to work, it was merely your own choice. But if you and your fellow workers agreed together to stay away, it became a crime.The true cruelty of this charge is It criminalized solidarity.

## Conclusion 

From the enclosure riots and the burning of Broom Hall in **1791**, through the Chartist struggles of the **1830s** and the dark violence of the **Sheffield Outrages (1860s)**, to the ultimate legal breakthrough of the **Conspiracy and Protection of Property Act in 1875**, it took roughly **80 to 90 years** of relentless friction to forge modern labor rights. 

> As E.P. Thompson observed in the book making of english working class, the working class made itself just as much as it was made. Enclosures, industrial revolution, and the Master and Servant Act shaped them, but history tells only half the story. Long before the legal breakthroughs, Sheffield workers were actively teaching themselves how to self-organize: building mutual aid societies, campaigning to abolish slavery and liberate chimney boys, founding independent newspapers to spread their ideas, drafting democratic constitutions, and **managing their own strike and cooperative treasuries**.
> 
> They forged these tools of self-governance with their own hands—and then sustained the struggle for eighty years, even though almost none of them lived to see it finally pay off.

From an isolated 'I'—the proud Little Mester stamping his name into a single blade—to a collective 'We' standing shoulder-to-shoulder against the machine, Sheffield's history reminds us **how the  working class was truly born and learned to survive through the hardship.**

## The Gunpowder and the Gig Economy

It is late at night on October 8th, 1866, on Hereford Street in Sheffield, when someone quietly walks up to a working man's house and drops a canister of gunpowder straight down his chimney. The entire house blows apart in a deafening roar, and yet the man living inside—a saw grinder named Thomas Fearnehough—somehow manages to crawl out of the rubble alive. By the time the sun comes up, this explosion is national news, leaving the whole country asking what kind of hired thugs or ruthless business rivals would try to murder a working man in his own bed.

But if you assume this was a hit ordered by a greedy boss, you would be entirely wrong, because the men who dropped that gunpowder were his own people, breathing the same iron dust and walking the same streets. It was his own union. To understand how a beautiful word like "solidarity" mutated into "agree with us or die," we have to rewind the clock and look at a city built by stubborn men who originally wanted absolutely nothing to do with each other.

Early Sheffield was essentially the world’s first gig economy with furnaces, where "Little Mesters" rented their own space at the grinding wheel, bought their own tools, and answered to no one but themselves. Thanks to a 1624 law that granted every trained apprentice a personal stamp to hammer into their steel, these craftsmen might have lived in a single dusty room, but they had a name that would outlive them in metal. They were fiercely independent, proud, and solitary, which begs the ultimate question: what exactly forces an every-man-for-himself crowd to swallow their pride and realize the guy covered in dust next to them is fighting the exact same war?

## Practicing Rebellion

The first cracks in that fierce individualism didn't actually happen at the grinding wheel, but rather in the pews of independent churches where the people of Sheffield were learning how to flat-out refuse the authority of the Church of England. Because they had practiced saying "no" to God's self-appointed managers, they already knew the political moves when it was time to take on the British Parliament, and the first time they flexed that muscle wasn't even for their own benefit.

Even though Sheffield's economy was heavily fed by the transatlantic slave trade—forging the very blades and hoes used on West Indian plantations—eight hundred disenfranchised metalworkers signed a massive petition in 1789 demanding the total abolition of the slave trade. That is what political awakening truly looks like: realizing that human rights must exist for everyone, even when defending those rights means actively smashing the bowl on your own kitchen table.

This defiant solidarity only deepened when the French Revolution erupted across the Channel, prompting the entire city of Sheffield—not just some fringe radical faction, but the whole town from top to bottom—to pour into the streets in massive celebration. Working men who couldn't even vote began reading Thomas Paine at the grinding wheel and calling each other "Citizen," inventing a unified political identity for themselves precisely because the state refused to give them one. But when they respectfully sent a petition for universal suffrage with ten thousand signatures to London, Parliament didn't just reject it; they dismissed it as "insolent," slamming the political door in their faces and forcing the workers to look for much darker avenues.

## Trapped on the Slope

After decades of having no official voice, the passage of the 1832 Reform Act gave Sheffield its own Members of Parliament, but the cruel irony was that the strict property requirements meant the overwhelming majority of workers still couldn't cast a ballot. This hollow victory fueled the Chartist movement, culminating in 1838 when twenty thousand desperate people crammed onto the quiet, cobbled slope of Paradise Square to demand a secret ballot and a vote for every man, only to have the authorities declare the assembly illegal and flood the streets with troops.

With the legal roads blockaded by soldiers and their peaceful assemblies banned, the movement was forced underground, leading a young man named Samuel Holberry to plan a desperate uprising in 1840 to seize the town hall. Because they were betrayed from within by a fellow worker who handed their plans straight to the chief constable, the rising was dead before it even started, and Holberry was sent to perish from tuberculosis in a prison cell at just twenty-seven years old. When their petitions were inevitably thrown back in their faces, their underground uprisings violently betrayed from within, and their strikes ruthlessly crushed by the state, the concept of a unified "we" had nowhere left to grow, forcing their identity to shrink dangerously from a national movement down to a viciously protected trade.

## The Bloody Price of Unity

Now we can finally understand why a union would bomb one of its own workers: when the government strips away your ability to be a citizen, all you have left to protect is your identity as a saw grinder. Because Sheffield was a city of little bosses where undercutting prices in secret meant the honest men would starve, the union couldn't rely on gentle persuasion; they had to enforce their rules through absolute terror, prioritizing one terrifying goal above all else.

They strictly limited who was allowed into the trade to keep their wages high, leading to the darkest tragedy of all: when ventilation technology was invented that would stop grinders from slowly drowning in their own stone dust, the workers fought against it, willingly trading their own lungs just to keep the job deadly and the door firmly shut to outsiders. This desperate gatekeeping escalated from cutting drive belts in the dead of night, to paying assassins fifteen pounds to shoot men who took on too many apprentices, and finally, to dropping a canister of gunpowder down Thomas Fearnehough's chimney.

"We" did not naturally emerge because a group of men woke up one morning feeling warm and united; rather, it was a brutal rule enforced in the dark, dragged into a public courtroom during the 1867 Royal Commission, and eventually converted into the legal union protections we know today. The tragedy that truly lingers is that the brave individuals who signed those petitions, starved in those strikes, and rotted in those prison cells never lived to see their radical demands become the perfectly ordinary reality we take for granted every time we cast a ballot.
