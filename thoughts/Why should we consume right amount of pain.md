In today’s world, high-level releases of dopamine are available at the touch of a button. Scrolling short-form videos, partying, gaming—pleasure has become an expectation of daily life. We tend to shelter ourselves in comfort, deeply reluctant to push to the other side of the scale: pain. But what is the true cost of chasing constant pleasure?

The Roman Stoic philosopher Seneca warned that the more pleasures a person captures, the more masters they will have to serve. Modern neuroscience backs him up.

In _Dopamine Nation_, psychiatrist Dr. Anna Lembke explains that our brains process pleasure and pain on a balance. When we experience intense pleasure, the scale tips to the pleasure side. The faster and harder it tips, the more intense the sensation. But the brain demands _homeostasis_—a baseline of physiological equilibrium. To restore balance, it triggers a compensatory mechanism to suppress that pleasure. Lembke uses a striking metaphor: neurochemical "gremlins" hop onto the pain side of the scale to counteract the weight of pleasure.

The catch? These gremlins don’t just jump off once the scale levels out. They stick around, tipping the balance into pain. This is the neurobiological reason we experience a hangover after a night of drinking, or a sense of emptiness and anxiety after hours of scrolling. As the Opponent-Process Theory of Emotion and Motivation states: _any prolonged or repeated departure from hedonic neutrality carries a cost._

So why should we deliberately walk toward pain?

Consider the "runner’s high." The act of running is challenging—often uncomfortable—yet it leaves us in a state of deep calm and clarity. Lembke shares the story of a patient recovering from severe addiction who struggled with profound emotional void. He discovered that daily ice baths provided a controlled, temporary pain that relieved his distress, triggering a natural, euphoric state essential to his recovery. As he put it: the cold water reminded him that simply being alive could feel good.

Through intermittent exposure to pain, our pleasure-pain balance adjusts. Over time, it gets weighted toward pleasure, rendering us far less vulnerable to suffering and far more capable of genuine joy.

This modern science aligns  with ancient Stoic philosophy. As William B. Irvine notes in _A Guide to the Good Life: The Ancient Art of Stoic Joy_, Stoics practiced "voluntary discomfort"—deliberately welcoming hardship to heighten their appreciation of life. They chose to endure cold or hunger even when warmth and food were readily available. Think of voluntary discomfort as a mental vaccine: by exposing ourselves to a small, controlled stressor now, we build an immunity that protects us from debilitating suffering in the future.

Furthermore, abstaining from short-term pleasure can itself become a source of profound satisfaction. Imagine you're on a diet and craving the ice cream sitting in your freezer. If you eat it, you get a quick burst of taste, immediately followed by regret. But if you refrain, you forgo that momentary pleasure to unlock a richer one. As Epictetus observed, you will _“be pleased and will praise yourself”_ for choosing self-mastery.

When tempted by an attractive object, do not let yourself be swept away. Tell yourself: _‘Wait for me a little; let me see what you are and what you represent; let me test you.’_

Because when you resist, what a sense of victory. What a sense of self-approval.
