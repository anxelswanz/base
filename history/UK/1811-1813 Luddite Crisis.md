
/ˈlʌdaɪt/

Luddites refers to the traditional artisans (such as hand-loom weavers, croppers, and stockingers) in Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire, and Lancashire, England, who took part in machine-breaking movements between 1811 and 1816.

They were not opposed to technology itself, but rather to capitalists using machinery to depress wages, employ child labor, produce shoddy goods, and thereby strip traditional artisans of their economic independence and human dignity. For them, breaking machines was a last-resort bargaining chip—a way to fight for their survival by destroying the capitalists' assets.

The government deployed over 12,000 troops to northern England for suppression. In 1812, the British Parliament passed the Frame Breaking Act, making the destruction of machinery a capital offense punishable by death. Subsequently, numerous Luddites were publicly hanged or transported to Australia. By 1813, the movement had been largely and bloodily suppressed.
